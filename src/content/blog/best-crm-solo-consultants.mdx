# Best CRM for Solo Consultants in 2026 (You Might Not Need One)

At some point in your first year as an independent consultant, you'll have a week where you forget to follow up with a warm lead, lose track of which proposal is waiting on which client, or double-book a discovery call because you were tracking everything in your head. Your first instinct is to go buy "a CRM" — because that's what real businesses have, right?

Here's the problem: almost every CRM on the market was designed for a sales team with a manager who needs pipeline visibility across multiple reps. You are one person with, realistically, somewhere between 5 and 40 active relationships at any given time. Buying HubSpot's full sales suite or a Salesforce seat to manage that is like renting a warehouse to store a bicycle.

This guide compares the tools solo consultants actually use to track clients and leads — including the option of not using a dedicated CRM at all — with real 2026 pricing and the actual limitations of each, not the marketing version.

## Do you actually need a CRM?

Before comparing tools, run this quick gut check:

If you have fewer than 15 active clients and prospects at once, and your main problem is "I forget to follow up," you don't need CRM software — you need a follow-up system, which a spreadsheet or a Notion page can give you for free. If you have 15–50 relationships in different stages (lead, proposal sent, negotiating, active client, past client you should re-engage), a lightweight CRM starts paying for itself in saved mental overhead. Past 50, or if you're bringing on a VA or subcontractor who needs shared visibility into your pipeline, a real CRM with permissions and automation becomes worth the monthly cost.

Most solo consultants overestimate which bucket they're in. Start cheap and upgrade when the spreadsheet visibly breaks, not before.

## The spreadsheet (still the right answer for most solo consultants)

Before you spend a dollar, try a Google Sheet with one tab and these columns: Name, Company, Status (Lead / Proposal Sent / Negotiating / Client / Past Client), Last Contact Date, Next Follow-Up Date, Notes. Sort by "Next Follow-Up Date" every Monday morning. That's the entire system that most CRM software is trying to replicate with extra steps.

The honest limitation: nothing reminds you to open the sheet. If you're not disciplined about a weekly review, leads quietly go cold and you won't notice until they've moved on. It also doesn't scale past a few dozen active relationships before the single-tab format gets unwieldy. But for a brand-new practice, this costs nothing and takes ten minutes to set up — don't let a shiny CRM demo talk you into skipping it.

## Notion: if you already live there

If you're already using Notion for proposals, meeting notes, or a client wiki, extending it into a lightweight CRM with a database, linked relations, and a Kanban-style pipeline view is a natural next step rather than a new tool to learn.

Notion's Free plan is genuinely usable for a solo consultant — unlimited pages and blocks, and enough file upload allowance for most day-to-day use. Plus is $10 per member per month billed annually and adds things like unlimited file uploads and custom sites, which mostly matter once you're collaborating with someone else.

The real limitation: Notion has no concept of email sync, automated follow-up reminders, or sequences. You can build a "Next Follow-Up" date property and a filtered view that surfaces overdue items, but nothing pings you outside of Notion itself — you still have to open the app and look. It's a database with good UI, not a sales tool.

## Airtable: more structure, more automation, more cost creep

Airtable is Notion's more spreadsheet-native cousin — better suited if you want multiple linked tables (Clients, Projects, Invoices) with real relational structure, plus filtered views and basic automations like "send me a Slack message when a deal sits in 'Proposal Sent' for 7 days."

The Free plan allows 1,000 records per base and 1GB of attachment storage per base, which is plenty for a client list, but automations are locked out entirely on Free — they only unlock on the Team plan at $20 per seat per month billed annually. That's the real cost trap: the feature that makes Airtable more useful than a spreadsheet is exactly the feature you don't get until you pay.

## Folk: built specifically for relationship-based businesses

Folk is the newer entrant that's picked up a following among consultants, freelancers, and agency owners specifically because it's built around relationships rather than sales pipelines — it pulls in contact data from LinkedIn, Gmail, and your existing network instead of asking you to manually enter everyone.

Standard is $24 per seat per month billed annually (or $30 month-to-month) and includes pipeline management, email campaigns, and contact enrichment. The real limitation is that Folk has no free plan at all — you get a 14-day trial and then you're paying, which is a harder sell for a brand-new practice with no revenue yet. The genuinely useful automation and custom-object features also don't kick in until Premium at $48 per seat per month, roughly double the entry price.

## Pipedrive: the closest thing to a "real" CRM at solo-consultant scale

Pipedrive is a genuine, purpose-built sales CRM with a visual pipeline that's easy to read at a glance — drag a deal from "Proposal Sent" to "Negotiating" and it just works, without you building the view yourself.

Lite starts at $14 per seat per month billed annually, which is the cheapest true CRM on this list. But the Lite plan explicitly excludes email sync and any workflow automation — both are reserved for the Growth plan at $39 per seat per month. That's a meaningful jump, and it means the entry-level Pipedrive plan is really just a nicer-looking version of the spreadsheet: you still have to remember to check it and update it by hand.

## HubSpot CRM: free, but the free plan is a funnel

HubSpot's free CRM is legitimately generous on paper — unlimited users listed as free for up to 2, up to 1,000 contacts, and a genuinely usable set of deal-tracking and contact-management tools with no time limit on the free tier.

The catch is that HubSpot's business model depends on you eventually needing more than the free plan offers, and it's built to nudge you there — HubSpot branding stays on your forms and some emails until you upgrade, and you'll hit the 1,000-contact ceiling faster than you'd expect once you're counting every lead, past client, and industry contact you've ever emailed. If you're a consultant who might eventually want marketing email and landing pages in the same system as your CRM, HubSpot's ecosystem is worth knowing about even if you don't need it on day one.

## What we'd actually pick

For a brand-new practice under 15 relationships: the spreadsheet, genuinely. Add a Notion database once you outgrow it, since you're likely using Notion for proposals anyway and it keeps everything in one workspace — see [ClickUp vs Notion for Solo Consultants](/blog/clickup-vs-notion-solo-consultants/) if you're deciding between the two for that job. If you're past 20–30 active relationships and pipeline visibility is costing you real deals — a forgotten follow-up that would have closed — Pipedrive Growth or Folk Premium are the two worth trialing, in that order, depending on whether you think in sales-pipeline terms (Pipedrive) or relationship terms (Folk).

## FAQ

**Do I need a CRM if I only have a handful of clients at a time?**

No. If you can name every active lead and client from memory right now, a CRM won't add anything a well-organized spreadsheet doesn't already give you. Revisit the question when tracking relationships in your head starts causing missed follow-ups.

**Is a free CRM plan actually usable long-term, or is it just a trial?**

Notion's and Airtable's free plans are genuinely usable indefinitely for solo use — they're not time-limited trials. HubSpot's free plan is also permanent, but it's deliberately capped and branded in ways designed to push you toward a paid tier as your contact list grows.

**Can I just use Gmail or Outlook instead of a separate CRM?**

You can track a small number of relationships using labels and a well-organized inbox, but email clients don't give you a pipeline view or a single place to see "who am I supposed to follow up with this week" — which is usually the actual problem a CRM is meant to solve.

**What's the cheapest tool here that includes real automation?**

Airtable's Team plan at $20 per seat per month is the cheapest option on this list that unlocks automation, though Pipedrive Growth at $39 per seat per month gives you automation built specifically around a sales pipeline rather than a general-purpose database.
